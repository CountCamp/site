The judgement of Solomon

dissect the design

You gave a therapy, and quality of life went up. Now you want to show that the therapy did that. But a sceptic has another explanation ready in no time: it was just the passing of time, it was that questionnaire beforehand, it only worked because they had already been thinking about it. That is what the Solomon four-group design (Richard L. Solomon, 1949) is for: four groups, one therapy, and in the improvement of each group different pieces: what gets better by itself (history and maturation, H/M), what the therapy does (the treatment, X), what the pretest itself does (the testing effect, T), and what pretest and therapy do together on top of that (pretest sensitization, S). You take them apart, one piece per step, until only S is left. That way you get there before the sceptic does: you can say exactly what you corrected for.

The design

A group of people gets a therapy that is meant to improve their quality of life. You measure that quality of life with a questionnaire; the score runs from 0 to 100, and higher is better. Random assignment divides the people into four groups. Two groups also fill in the questionnaire before the therapy: the pretest. All four fill it in at the end: the posttest. The table shows the mean score per group.

And in your own research?

If you measure before and after in your own research, the improvement you see holds everything at once: what gets better by itself, your intervention, and what the pretest itself did. With one group you cannot take those pieces apart. For that you need groups that differ in one thing only, the way II and IV here differ only in the pretest. So for every effect you want to know, look for the two groups that yield it. If you cannot find them in your design, you cannot see that effect on its own either.

And that is what you can tell the sceptic: “it got better by itself” you have answered as soon as you have a group without therapy, “it was the questionnaire” only once you also have a group without a pretest. Whatever your design cannot answer, you say so yourself, before they ask.

And S is a difference in differences: the effect of the therapy on quality of life with a pretest, minus the effect of the therapy on quality of life without a pretest. That is called an interaction, and that is what chapter 10 of the (Dutch) book, Verschil in verschil, is about.

For those who want more: how do you test whether S really differs from zero?

This page works with means, with the borrowed pretest added. That is how Solomon (1949) worked it out himself, and to find S that is fine. If you also want to test whether S really differs from zero, you need more than means: you look at the scores of all the people. There you get stuck, because groups III and IV have no pretest of their own, so no improvement per person either. Campbell and Stanley (1963) therefore recommended another route: test on the posttests, with two factors: pretest (yes or no) and therapy (yes or no), the two switches from the encore. That is exactly the regression on the posttest from the encore, and the interaction in it is S. If the pretests of I and II are equal, it gives the same number as the calculations.
