Table D · the t-distribution

critical values for a test and for an interval

The table gives the critical value t*t^*. To its right lies probability p: you find that at the top, in the column heading. Between −t*-t^* and t*t^* lies C: you find that at the bottom.

A split in two at the top, a split in three at the bottom

You can read table D from two sides.

  • At the top is a split in two: probability p in one tail, to the right of t*t^*.
  • At the bottom is a split in three: the middle C, with a tail on each side.

For an interval you think in splits in three, and then you look at the bottom. Both lead to the same cell: if the middle C = 95%, a probability of .95, then .05 is left over for the two tails together, and that is .025 per tail.

t*t^* for an interval

In week 3 you made a 95% interval for all 30 poor sleepers, with df = 30 − 1 = 29.

  • At the bottom at 95%, then up to row 29: 2.045.
  • At the top you end up in the same column: (1 − .95)/2 = .025.

That is the t*t^* from week 3. See Figure 1.

WarningTwo-tailed with α = .05 is not the .05 column

If you test two-tailed with α = .05, you split that .05 over two tails: .025 per tail. So you look in the .025 column, or at the bottom at 95%: the same number. The .05 column belongs to one tail, and to a 90% interval.

Bracketing a p

The table doesn’t give a p for every t. But you can bracket the p between two columns.

In week 2 you found t = 2.45 for the sleep study, with df = 29. In row 29, 2.45 lies between 2.150 (column .02) and 2.462 (column .01). So:

  • one tail: .01 < p < .02;
  • two tails, times two: then p lies between .02 and .04.

Watch the order: a bigger t goes with a smaller p. Figure 2 shows why: your tail, to the right of 2.45, is smaller than the one to the right of 2.150 and bigger than the one to the right of 2.462. R gave p = .021, and that lies neatly between them.

Beyond the last column. For the paired test in week 3, t was −4.52, with df = 14. The minus sign doesn’t matter: the curve is symmetric, so look up 4.52. The biggest number in row 14 is 4.140, at .0005, and 4.52 lies even further out. So:

  • one tail: smaller than .0005;
  • two tails, times two: p < .001.

Figure 3 shows it: your stroke is beyond the last line the table knows. Table D doesn’t go further than .0005.

If your df isn’t in the table

After 30 the table skips: 40, 50, 60, 80, 100 and 1000. If your df isn’t in it, take the next smaller one that is. For df = 44 that is 40. The same goes for a df with decimals, like the 27.95 that R gives for Welch in week 3: take 27, not 28. Without R you don’t know that 27.95; then, as in week 3, you take the lower bound, the smaller of n1−1n_1 - 1 and n2−1n_2 - 1 (here 14), and you are a little more cautious still.

Why the smaller one? The fewer degrees of freedom, the fatter the tails (week 2), and the further out t*t^* lies. At df = 40, t*t^* = 2.021; at exactly df = 44 it would be 2.015. So your interval gets a little too wide, never too narrow. And if you reject with the smaller df, R rejects with the real df as well. That is the cautious side, the same as with the lower bound for Welch in week 3.

The row z*z^*

Under row 1000 is the row z*z^*: the t-distribution with infinitely many degrees of freedom, and that is the normal distribution. That is where the three anchors from week 0 are, to three decimal places: 1.645 (column .05), 1.960 (column .025) and 2.576 (column .005). Table A doesn’t have 1.645 exactly; this row does.

NoteComparing with another edition of this table?

In some editions the row z*z^* has 0.841 where this one has 0.842, and 3.091 where this one has 3.090. Those two are rounded wrongly there: qnorm(.80) is 0.8416 and qnorm(.999) is 3.0902. Here every number is calculated in R.
