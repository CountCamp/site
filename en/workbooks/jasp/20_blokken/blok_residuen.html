Data for this exercise — stones.csv the twelve stones from the book · penguins.csv 342 penguins · bigfive.csv 2634 completed questionnaires · what each column means

hook

Twenty-two penguins of exactly 190 millimetres

prediction · miss

The penguins are old acquaintances — in Exercise 3.2 you drew a line through them yourself: the weight predicted from the flipper length, nearly fifty grams of penguin per millimetre of flipper, and R² = .76.1 Three quarters of all the difference in weight runs along with the flipper. That’s a lot, for living animals, and it sounds as if the line now more or less knows the weight.

And now something odd. The file holds twenty-two penguins with a flipper of exactly 190 millimetres. The line knows only the flipper length, so all twenty-two get the same prediction: 3659.4 grams. What the scales actually said, in grams:

3050 · 3325 · 3400 · 3450 · 3450 · 3450 · 3575 · 3600 · 3600 · 3650 · 3700 · 3700 · 3725 · 3800 · 3900 · 3900 · 3900 · 3950 · 4250 · 4250 · 4250 · 4600

From 3050 to 4600 — a kilo and a half of difference, at one and the same flipper length. The line can do nothing about that: it knows just one thing about a penguin, and that one thing was the same twenty-two times over.

And those twenty-two are no oddity you just happened to hit on. Pick another flipper length and you see the same thing, only with fewer animals in a huddle at once: all along the line, reality sits above it and below it. That sharpens a question we’ve left to one side until now: how far is the line off, penguin by penguin — and what can you do with those misses?

Come along for a moment. The line has had its guess; now we mark its work.

play with it

Work out the misses on the twelve stones

residual · predicted value

You’ve already seen a miss like that written down in the book: DATA = FIT + RESIDUAL. The fit is what the line guesses, the residual is what’s left over — the miss, now no longer measured from the mean but from the line.

You’ll come across this one thing under four names: miss, residual, deviation, and, in software output and in articles, error. Each time it’s the distance between what is really there and what the line guessed. In this exercise it’s called the miss; when JASP says Residual or a plot says residual, it’s exactly the same thing.

NoteFirst as in the book — the twelve stones · JASP 0.99.0

Open stones.csv and make two columns in the R box (type Scale; after the name Create Column, after the line Compute column), as in the toolbox: left of the arrow the name of the column, right of it what goes in the R box.

guess      ->   fitted(lm(shine ~ carat))
residual   ->   resid(lm(shine ~ carat))

Copy the right-hand side exactly; you don’t have to read it. The top one says “give me what the line guesses”, the bottom one “give me what is left over”. Rounded to one decimal:

guess       30.7   33.9   33.9   40.4   50.0   50.0   53.2   56.4   59.6   59.6   66.1   66.1
residual   -10.7   -3.9   -3.9  -10.4    0.0   20.0   26.8    3.6   10.4   20.4  -36.1  -16.1

Then add up residual: Descriptives, residual in Variables, and under Statistics the Sum tick box.

Check whether that sum comes to zero for you too — give or take a little rounding dust. If it doesn’t, somewhere along the way you’ve got hold of something other than the residuals of this model.

Those are exactly the two columns from the table in the book: Else lands on zero (the line runs straight through her), Kees is furthest off, at −36.1. New is only the picture below — you set those two columns against each other instead of reading them:

NoteIn JASP · the misses in a picture · JASP 0.99.0

Run Regression → Linear Regression with shine as Dependent Variable and carat in Covariates, and under Plots tick the option Residuals vs. predicted: the guess on the horizontal axis, the miss on the vertical one, and the zero line straight through it.

Look closely at what that picture does. The regression line has gone flat: everything the line already knew has been calculated out, so what’s left over should hover around the zero line. A residual plot is a scatterplot swept clean — you’re no longer looking at the association, you’re looking at what the association left behind.

CautionAnswer — open after your own attempt

a) Shine itself spreads with SD = 21.74 shine points, the residuals with SD = 17.75. Without the line your best guess for every stone is the mean, and how widely the misses fan out around that mean is what the standard deviation of shine sums up: nearly 22 shine points. Guess with the line, and the residuals fan out around the line instead, and that fan is narrower: nearly 18. So the line does save you something, but not much — with these twelve stones, carat is nowhere near everything.

Don’t confuse that 17.75 with the 18.61 from Exercise 3.2, the number the regression itself prints for the spread around the line. Both come from the same squares of the residuals, the ones you add up in b). A standard deviation divides them by eleven, n − 1, because the mean costs one number; the 18.61 divides by ten, n − 2, because the line costs two (the intercept and the slope). Both then take the square root. In the book that second one is called the standard error of the estimate, Se. If someone asks for the standard error of the estimate, it’s that one: 18.61.

NoteIn JASP

Descriptives with shine and residual: Std. Deviation 21.742 and 17.746.

The 18.61 from Exercise 3.2 is in the Model Summary of your regression, under RMSE: 18.613.

b) Shine around its own mean gives 5200.0; shine around the line gives 3464.3. So of all the difference in shine, two thirds is left over once the line has had its share — and the one third it does take is exactly the R² = .33 you saw in Exercises 3.1 and 3.2.

NoteIn JASP

Why that is so, you’ll find below.

get it

The residual, and where R² comes from

residual · residual plot · R²

Look at that sum of the residuals once more. Zero — not roughly, but exactly, give or take rounding. That’s no coincidence and no compliment to the line either: it’s the construction. That’s how the line was laid — for every miss on the plus side there’s just as much on the minus side, because if anything were left over, the whole line could shift a bit, and then it wouldn’t have been the best-fitting line.

Build in small moments to check — it ought to be zero; is it? So do hold on to that zero as a checkpoint: if your column of residuals doesn’t add up to zero, something has gone wrong along the way.

But mind what it also means. Precisely because that sum is always zero — with a line that runs tight through the cloud and with a line that leaves all sorts behind — it says nothing about how well the line guesses. Plus and minus cancel out regardless. If you want to know how well, you mustn’t add up but look: how widely do the misses lie around zero?

And that’s exactly what you see in the residual plot. The twenty-two this exercise opened with stand there as a single column: same guess, twenty-two misses, above and below zero.

NoteIn JASP · draw the misses and count the garden · JASP 0.99.0

Open penguins.csv and run Regression → Linear Regression with body_mass_g as Dependent Variable and flipper_length_mm in Covariates; under Plots, tick Residuals vs. predicted. Then make the same column as with the stones:

residual   ->   resid(lm(body_mass_g ~ flipper_length_mm))

Descriptives gives you the two numbers you need: body_mass_g spreads with Std. Deviation 801.955, residual with 393.700. Divide one by the other and square, in three small steps you can all check by hand:

393.700 / 801.955 = 0.491
0.491 x 0.491     = 0.241
1 - 0.241         = .759

Mind one number in Model Summary: there it says RMSE 394.278 and not 393.700. Neither is wrong — the RMSE divides by 340 (342 animals minus the two numbers that describe the line, the intercept and the slope), a standard deviation by 341.

Without the line your best guess for every weight was the mean — you know that game. The standard deviation of the weight measures how widely the weights lie around that one guess: 801.95 grams. With the line they lie closer together. The standard deviation of the misses measures how widely they lie around the line: 393.70 grams. So the line makes the spread 408.25 grams narrower, just over half of the spread without the line.

And yet Exercise 3.2 said R² = .76. Three quarters. Who’s lying — the half or the three quarters? Neither; they count in a different measure. Think of all the difference in weight as a garden full of variation. Explaining an association isn’t explaining a length but an area, and area goes in squares — so square the two standard deviations before you divide one by the other. Then it says .24: the part of the garden the line leaves behind, divided by the whole garden. The rest the line does take: one minus .24 is .76. In metres along the edge the line wins just over half; in garden area, three quarters. It’s the same gain, measured twice.

And R² needs nothing new for that: everything it knows is already in the misses. Do the same on the twelve stones and out comes .33 — exactly the square of the r you put together yourself at the start of this chapter, with all its decimals (the rounded .58 squared gives .34). Two routes, one number: square the correlation, or keep the books on the misses.

sum of the residuals · residual plot · R² from the misses

NoteYour turn

Answer the questions in order; they build on each other. The last one is deliberately called Z) and not k): the cloud question always comes last.

There are eleven of them, and that’s too many for one sitting. After e) you have the misses in hand and you’ve found the hook again — a good moment to stop. At f) the bookkeeping starts, and that’s the part R² rolls out of.

And that’s the point of this exercise. R² is not a separate measure that JASP conjures up from somewhere: it’s the garden the line takes, divided by the whole garden. You’ve just worked it out from the misses yourself.

j) For example:

The line of weight on flipper length covered just over three quarters of the differences in weight among 342 penguins (R² = .76), but still left nearly four hundred grams of spread behind per animal (SD of the residuals = 393.70).

Z) Half and half — and the half that doesn’t hold, you know already.

There’s a slight arc in it. A slanting stripe can’t be in it: the association between the guess and the miss is exactly zero, and that’s no finding but the construction — that’s precisely what “the line has gone flat” means. An arc can. Look carefully at your plot from c): at the low and the high guesses the points lie on average a little above the zero line, in the middle on average below it. That’s the dip from Exercise 3.2 — +57, −106 and +41 grams per third of flipper length — now as a picture instead of as three numbers. You can barely see it, and that’s why 3.2 worked it out for you. Where it comes from, you know already too: from the species. At i) the line sat a good two hundred grams too high for the Chinstraps, on average.

And no penguin pulls on the line all by itself. The biggest miss is 1288.69 grams, and that’s a good three times the standard deviation of the residuals — an animal that stands out, but not one that tips the line. Leave it out and the slope barely moves.

NoteIn JASP

Regression → Correlation with guess and residual: Pearson’s r 0.000 — exactly zero, give or take machine noise.

What hangs on it: little here, because the arc is slight. Had it been stronger, the whole bookkeeping would still stand — the sums at f), the R² at g), the standard deviations at h) and the species means at i) are sums over these 342 animals, and those stay right whatever is bent. What you would then redo is every sentence in which you recommend the line: at j) it says it covers three quarters, and if the association runs strongly curved, that’s no lie, but it is a line that lets you down in the wrong place.

Look at what that second sentence does. Everything you’ve worked out in this chapter — the covariance, the correlation, the line, the residuals, the R² — describes the people in front of you, and nobody else. You’ve been given no reason at all to assume that the next 2634 people would show the same, and no way either of saying how likely that is.

That is exactly where the next movement begins.

exam practice

Exam-style questions

DATA = FIT + RESIDUAL · R² from the misses · residual plot

These six have the form an exam would ask this topic in: four options, and exactly one is the best — the other three may well be true and still not be the best answer. Only questions 2 and 3 need any arithmetic.

NoteExam-style questions

1. You add up all 342 misses of the line and zero comes out. What may you conclude from that about how well this line guesses?

  1. Nothing: with every best-fitting line that sum is zero, even with a line that’s completely off.
  2. The line fits well: the misses above and below the line keep each other neatly in balance.
  3. The R² must be 1, because on balance nothing is left over.
  4. The line guesses neither too high nor too low on average, and that’s exactly what you want from a good line.

2. One of the twenty-two penguins with a flipper of 190 millimetres weighs 4250 grams. The line predicts 3659.4 grams for all twenty-two. How big is the miss for this animal?

  1. −590.6 grams
  2. 590.6 grams
  3. 4250.0 grams
  4. 7909.4 grams

3. For the line of weight on flipper length, the total sum of the squared deviations is 219 307 697 and the residual sum 52 854 796. What is the R² of this line?

  1. .24
  2. .51
  3. .76
  4. 3.15

4. The standard deviation of the weight is 801.95 grams, that of the misses 393.70 grams. In grams, then, the line makes the spread 51 per cent narrower, while the R² is .76. Which statement about those two numbers is the best?

  1. One of the two must be wrong; the same gain can’t have two numbers.
  2. The 51 per cent counts in grams and the .76 in squared grams; it’s the same gain, measured twice.
  3. The .76 belongs to the line and the 51 per cent to the misses, so they’re about different things.
  4. The .76 is too optimistic: 51 per cent is what the line really saves you, and the R² inflates that by squaring.

5. You draw the residual plot: the guess on one axis, the miss on the other. What should be there, and what does it mean if there is a pattern in it?

  1. A rising cloud — the miss should run along with the guess; if it lies flat, something has gone wrong.
  2. A shapeless cloud around the zero line; a pattern means the line has left something behind that it could have taken along.
  3. A shapeless cloud around the zero line; a pattern means there are measurement errors in the file.
  4. A falling cloud — big guesses always go with smaller misses.

6. You ask for the mean miss per species and it says: Adelie +43.5, Chinstrap −215.7 and Gentoo +65.8 grams. Yet the mean miss over all 342 animals is zero. Which statement is the best?

  1. Those can’t both be true: three means that aren’t zero can’t add up to zero between them.
  2. The line is useless for Chinstraps and must be drawn separately for that species.
  3. The species carries information the line doesn’t have: within a group the balance may tilt, even though over all 342 animals it stands at zero.
  4. The three species differ too much in size, and so the biggest species pulls the mean towards zero.

Below, for each question, is why — and why the other three options are tempting. If you got one right, you can skip it.

1. a. That zero is the construction and not the outcome: that’s how the line was laid. A number that’s always the same can’t tell you anything about what differs. b reads the balance as a compliment, and that’s the trap — plus and minus cancel out with a line that leaves all sorts behind, too. c confuses nothing on balance with nothing: the misses aren’t zero, their sum is zero, and the R² is .76. d is the trickiest, because the statement is true — the line guesses neither too high nor too low on average — and precisely because of that it says nothing: it holds for every best-fitting line, so it distinguishes this line from nothing.

2. b. DATA = FIT + RESIDUAL, so the miss is what’s really there minus what the line guessed: 4250 − 3659.4 = 590.6 grams. a has the sign the wrong way round and computes fit minus data; then this animal would be lighter than predicted, while it’s heavier. c names the weight itself. d adds instead of subtracting: 4250 + 3659.4 = 7909.4, and that’s heavier than the heaviest penguin in the file.

3. c. One minus the residual sum divided by the total sum: 1 − 52 854 796 / 219 307 697 = .76. a is the division without that one-minus in front — .24 is exactly the part the line does not take, and that’s exactly why the minus is there. b is the 51 per cent from question 4, the number in grams rather than in squared grams. d divides the fit sum by the residual sum: 166 452 902 / 52 854 796 = 3.15. A share coming out above 1 is your clue that something hasn’t been divided by the total.

4. b. Think of all the difference in weight as a garden: along the edge the line wins just over half, in area three quarters. Area goes in squares, so square first and then divide. a looks for a mistake where there is none; both numbers can be checked. c sets the line and the misses against each other, but they come from the same bookkeeping — the R² was worked out from the misses. d calls squaring an inflating move. It’s a choice of measure, and it’s the measure that explaining an association comes in: area, not length.

5. b. Everything the line already knew has been calculated out, so what’s left over may no longer carry any pattern — and if there is a pattern in it, then there was still something in the misses the line could have used. a and d expect a direction in the residual plot, and there can’t be one: with a best-fitting line the association between the guess and the miss is exactly zero. That the plot is flat is therefore no sign that something has gone wrong, but the intention. c has the right cloud and the wrong conclusion: a pattern points to something the model is missing, not to faults in the scales.

Note that pattern has two guises here, and you meet both. A pattern along the axis is an arc, or a fan that gets wider: then the miss goes with the height of the guess. A pattern per group is what you work out at i): within one species the misses lie on one side on average, while over all 342 animals together they come out at zero. The second you don’t see if you don’t colour the points — and that’s exactly why i) asks you to.

6. c. Over all 342 animals the mean miss is zero, because that’s how the line was laid — but within a group that balance is quite free to tilt. That the Chinstraps are systematically a good two hundred grams off means the species knows something the line doesn’t: the third player from 3.1, now visible in the misses. a is too quick: negative and positive do add up to zero between them, and these are unequal groups besides (151, 68 and 123 animals), so it doesn’t even count with the same number of animals per species. b jumps from an observation to an intervention; two hundred grams on an animal of a good three and a half kilos is small, and this exercise deliberately lets that line stand. d has the biggest species pulling the mean towards zero, and that’s the wrong way round: the zero isn’t there in spite of the species but by construction.
