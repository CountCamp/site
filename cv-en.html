CV

← Nederlandse versie

I’m a passionate teacher, methodologist, and researcher with a mission: making statistics and methodology accessible to people who initially think it’s not for them. My strength lies in translating complex subjects into clear and lasting insights.

I was fourteen when I started tutoring maths, so the teaching was in me long before anything official. It got serious during my psychology studies, back in 1997, when I began tutoring fellow students in mathematics, statistics and methodology. Psychology attracts people who are, let’s say, alpha-minded — drawn to human beings, not to formulas — and the technical, quantitative courses were exactly where they got stuck. What I discovered was not just that I could teach this material, but that I wanted to, more than almost anything else: finding the most parsimonious way to explain something hard, so that real, lasting insight forms. Watching students who were terrified at the start slowly begin to like the subject — that turned out to be one of my goals in life. The classes grew so quickly that I eventually left my other job, as a manager in a bar (where, honestly, the social and communication skills transferred to the classroom better than you’d expect), and in 2006 I officially founded my own company: WISI — Wiskunde & Statistiek Inzichtelijk, “Mathematics & Statistics Made Clear”.

The larger aim behind all of it is simple: better research skills and better decisions, in science and in daily life. Most people find research methods a dry matter. I like showing them otherwise — that tools like logic and statistics are things you can actually enjoy using, and that they improve the quality of your work and your thinking, whether you’re writing a thesis or just trying to make sense of the world.

Projects & advisory

For over 25 years now, the core of my work has been organizing and teaching university-level statistics courses — bachelor and master, up to and including multivariate methods — alongside workshops at conferences and advising on scientific research. That advisory work has ranged from doctoral projects at the university to research for public bodies such as the Fire Service (Brandweer) and the Hollands Midden safety region.

Over the years I’ve built close working relationships with Leiden University (psychology) and Hogeschool Leiden’s applied psychology programme. I teach at every level from the basics up to (research) master level, in classes of up to 75 students as well as one-on-one. One number I’m quietly proud of: of the roughly 500 students who start psychology in Leiden each year, about 100 choose my courses over the regular ones the university runs itself. I also work freelance for Hogeschool INHolland and Webster University in Leiden. At the university of applied sciences, my core role is smoothing the transition from secondary school to higher education — I know what students will need to master later on, so I can build toward it early.

The work has occasionally taken me far from a Dutch classroom. In Bangladesh, I advised on and carried out analyses for research in nature and wildlife development: I organized a methodology course for the researchers of the Wildlife Trust of Bangladesh, and I analyzed tiger data for Adam Barlow — one of the leading tiger experts, based in England — and advised him on his research. Teaching researchers who spend their days tracking tigers how to set up a sound study is, I can report, exactly as wonderful as it sounds.

And through all of it runs one constant: guiding PhD candidates in setting up sound research, so they can carry out their studies and write their theses with confidence in their own methods.
